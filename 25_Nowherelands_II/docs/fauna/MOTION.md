# Lumen shoal

Procedural luminous shoals for Nowherelands II. No imported creature assets are required.

Validation: 71 world/weather/lumen/guide tests pass. Lumen-only and full-prototype simulations match exactly for all 640 individuals over 120 seconds including a startled departure. The standalone browser build renders only lumen shoals; GPU deformation, surface-light and audio mix checks pass.

Lumen use refined wet/dry boundary crossings from generated lake basins. There is one persistent population of **640 individuals**, with six temporary navigation streams and one shared neighbour field. Recruitment at another lake returns the same population. One third are tiny (radius scale 0.13–0.35); others span 0.65–1.55. Sizes are shuffled across streams. Small individuals have quicker acceleration and faster local wandering. The six initial flocks contain 150, 36, 200, 58, 112 and 84 animals. They start at widely separated lakes using farthest-point placement, with a nearby flock retained for the first encounter. Where there are fewer than six lakes, visitors use separate shoreline patches. Settled visitors continue moving around their banks.

Each stream independently rests, escapes or plays, migrates and settles. A nearby player triggers staggered escape and a distinct rising liquid flutter with an airy release. Normal calls retain the liquid whistle. Escape cues reserve two mixer slots and stay spatially anchored at take-off so a fast departure does not immediately make the cue inaudible. Their foreground output bypasses ambient ducking while respecting the master volume, compressor and fauna mute; the alarm envelope peak is 0.46 with four fuller pulses over 1.6 seconds. Its foreground gain is 1.85 (ambient fauna remains 0.85), a 6.75 dB direct-signal increase over the prior mix. Accepted alarms briefly lower the ambient bus to 28%, returning over 1.8 seconds. Offline checks cover 25-unit proximity, 90% ambient ducking, the initial master fade and muted rejection. The checked escape RMS is 0.08566 across seven seconds with and without ambient ducking (peak 0.6390); a separate fixture uses the actual drone and wind voices, HRTF at 20 units, reverb and the master compressor. Its mixed peak is 0.936; the alarm-only and ducked-background renders measure 0.261 and 0.106 RMS respectively over the first second. These are signal checks, not subjective listening judgments in every possible mix. Autonomous departure makes no alarm. Observation alone does not scare animals.

Streams first play in the sky for 24–40 seconds, with no selected destination. The existing take-off guide trajectory is retained. A frightened individual first accelerates in a curved outward fan for 0.4 seconds, blending back into guide following by 1.4 seconds. Nearby animals respond within two simulation steps; the alarm reaches the entire flock in under 0.18 seconds. The old resting trail is bypassed during this burst, then individual following delays return smoothly over six seconds. Autonomous departures retain their previous steering. Stronger individual cross-flight blends in from six to fourteen seconds after departure. Highland launches ease their extra lift back down after the launch. Individuals follow different times along a continuously recorded guide path (0.15–4.2 seconds behind), but also continually overtake, cross and undulate through the flock. Position offsets and their velocity derivatives vary independently, so the animals do not hold fixed slots in a translated formation. Local alignment and cohesion weaken as the launch blends into active flight, while short-range separation discourages overlap. A global spatial grid selects up to seven nearby neighbours, regardless of stream membership, and excludes neighbours farther than 120 units. Distant streams cannot pull one another back together.

After play, each stream chooses a lake with the fewest visitors among its candidates; two streams prefer highland lakes. Routes curve through space and advance at cruise speed without waiting for the intentionally delayed followers. Height comes from the local terrain plus modest clearance, not interpolation between the source and destination lake elevations. Ridge anticipation raises the approaching path but no longer carries the previous peak across the valley. Individual swarm movement remains active during travel and mergers. A desired speed floor prevents prolonged airborne hovering, and bounded acceleration preserves fluid changes of direction. Every movement step checks actual body clearance against terrain. Near arrival, animals peel toward separate shoreline patches and slow only during their own landing approach; there is no requirement for the whole flock to assemble at one slow-moving arrival point. Nearby travelling flocks (within 750 units) can approach a shared moving corridor. They must physically converge before a 12–18 second mixing period begins. Their paths overlap while the individuals keep their delayed, wavering positions along the ribbon. They then split spatially into unequal flocks, with bounds to prevent a flock being swallowed over repeated mergers. When possible, former partners select different destination lakes. An approach that cannot converge within 28 seconds is abandoned without exchanging remote animals. No periodic global gathering is imposed. Reunions are opportunistic, so their frequency depends on the map and where paths cross.

Rendering uses identity lumen instance matrices. The shader receives interpolated world position, size and velocity directly, with no heading, pitch or bank. A directionless sphere undergoes symmetric stretch/squash of up to 50% along motion, with stronger surface waves and bounded surface displacement from velocity and elastic acceleration. There is no head or pointed tail. Motion and elastic acceleration gently shift cyan/blue/lavender colour and brightness, with independent phases. A shared bounded appearance signal drives the body, its soft halo and the colour of its surface illumination. The body radiance and halo are reduced for the denser unified flock; brightness is capped at 0.88 before the facing contribution. Eight stable nearby emitters also contribute finite-distance light to the custom terrain, rock and inland-water shaders, including water highlights. This is local illumination without shadow maps or full dynamic reflection of each creature.

### Pebble hopper camouflage and escape

Pebble hoppers now prefer hard, gently sloping ground and exposed gravel shores, with less vegetation and enough dry clearance for a whole body. Dense forest, marsh, soft sand, steep slopes and cave openings are unsuitable. Colonies start with three to six animals (fewer when placement or population capacity is constrained), among up to sixteen ordinary stones. Static stones and live shells share the world's rock material, including environmental light, grain, wetness and mineral bands. Shells have stable individual proportions, irregular facets, flattened undersides and soft contact shadows. They do not squash or breathe.

Undisturbed animals remain in place; occasional brief weight shifts are separated by long pauses. A nearby player makes them freeze, then rapidly unfold and run away. Ground-plane distance and the player's approach speed determine the reaction; the eleven-unit eye height does not prevent triggering. Observation cameras and high flight are ignored. Neighbors react with individual delays, with some holding their disguise. Ambient music neither exposes them through automatic calls nor prevents an escape.

Escapes favor nearby cover while retaining a preference for the home patch. Whole-body footprint checks sample the route, reject water/cliffs/physical obstacles, and avoid colony stones and neighbors. Short doglegs handle blocked direct routes. Animals slow before tight corners, brake with corrective steps, hesitate, lower their weight, and withdraw their legs under the shell. A later player approach can trigger another retreat. Where no safe short route exists, an animal retains its disguise.

The two tapered, jointed legs use fixed world-space contacts and alternating lifted recovery steps. The body's suspension, lean and small landing response follow foot support and acceleration. At full rest there are no rendered leg instances. Both pose and feet interpolate between the fixed simulation steps. `PebbleHoppers.js` owns behavior, habitat and feet; `PebbleMeshes.js` owns the rock shells, scenery stones, legs and contact shadows.

Use **Pebble hoppers → Approach the stones** in the field guide for an actual player encounter. The dedicated study provides whole-patch and close views, walking/running approaches, uneven/flat ground, retreat, reset, slow motion, pause and individual frame stepping.

### Watchful pebble eyes

Each animal now has two small snail-like eyes on curved, tapered stalks. Muted mineral colors keep them part of the stone silhouette, with dark directional pupils making their attention readable. At rest the eyes take independently timed peeks while the shell stays rigid and the legs remain hidden. Both eyes now keep following the player at every distance, including during observation, high flight, escape and regrouping. Their bulbs are now neutral white and twice the radius of the previous enlarged eyes. The curved stalks reach more than twice as high, with a wider outward spread and slightly thicker stems to support the larger bulbs. This makes the animals readable while walking past them. Within 27 ground units they extend farther to watch more intently. Startling retracts the stalks quickly and bends them back during the sprint. They emerge gradually during recovery, with more frequent checking for a few seconds after escape.

`PebbleEyes.js` owns gaze and extension on the fixed simulation clock, using a separate seeded random stream so looking around does not change movement randomness. `PebbleEyeMeshes.js` renders seven curved-stalk segments per eye, small bulbs and pupils with interpolated pose, world lighting and fog. Ordinary scenery stones have no eyes. The study includes eye state in its inspection data and checks the eye rig through 241 resting, fleeing and settling frames. Dedicated behavior regressions cover independent peeks, gaze across body headings and distant observation, fast retraction and cautious recovery.

### Scatter, regroup and cave colonies

An alarm assigns distinct directions across a broad fan. Open-ground sprint pace is now 46–56 units per second before individual size scaling; short turns and obstacles reduce it. Swing timing follows acceleration so an unfolding foot does not lag behind the first sprint strides. Rendered contact checks sample the actual bone endpoints; simulation contact tests run at 120 Hz to observe steps that can finish within a 30 Hz frame.

Regrouping becomes available 2.2 seconds after the alarm, once each animal finishes its escape. They remain upright and hurry to a shared safe patch at 21–26 units per second before size scaling. Early arrivals wait on their feet at separate reunion spots. The whole colony must arrive before any member starts folding its legs away. Reunion routes avoid the player, water, cliffs, stones and neighbors; short doglegs and a bounded grid search handle obstructions. Intercepting the return starts another colony-wide scatter. Tests cover a complete scatter–wait–reunion cycle across six seeds and a renewed approach during regrouping.

Explicit habitat sites seed colonies at cave entrances, on dry gallery floors, and beside lakes above 150 elevation units. Cave colonies sample their own floor layer, including merged cave intervals, wall clearance, headroom, local water and large cave rocks. The caves already contain rubble, so these colonies add no extra rings of camouflage stones that could block dry shelves. Narrow passages naturally constrain scattering distance and direction.
